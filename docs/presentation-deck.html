A briefing

Every figure in the case study is illustrative; no personnel data appears anywhere. Five parts: what the system is, what we want it to achieve, the methodology and the reasoning behind it, a worked example, and what it deliberately does not do.

What the system is

1

The taxonomy

278 named activities the laboratory can be asked to perform, each with written criteria at six levels of proficiency.

2

The record

One dated, attributable assessment per person per activity, holding the evidence it rests on and the authorization it carries.

3

The decisions

Who can staff a job, what each person needs next, and which services rest on a single qualified person.

The first two stages are discipline. The third is the one the laboratory actually opens every week.

Overview2

The taxonomy is reference data, versioned in the repository as JSON. We are not an accredited laboratory; we align the method with ISO/IEC 17025 because it is the established approach. The record is runtime data about people. The decisions are the reports: gap analysis, service readiness, project staffing.

Three questions, answered from one record

Who can we put on this work?

Asked every time an engagement lands, and again whenever somebody is unavailable. It has to be answerable before the work is promised, not after.

What makes us confident they can?

Asked by clients, by partners who rely on our results, and by us. A job title is not an answer; a dated assessment against written criteria is.

Where does this person go next?

Asked at every review, and by the person themselves. The answer should be a named set of skills and a date, not an adjective.

The laboratory is not accredited and this is not a compliance exercise. We align our competence method with ISO/IEC 17025 because it is the established answer to the second question, and holding ourselves to it makes the other two answerable too. The third is the one people in the lab care about most, and the one a job title answers worst.

What we want it to achieve

Staff projects

Every service we sell lists the skills it needs at the levels it needs them. Matching that against assessed people tells us who can take a job, and where only one person could.

Stand behind results

A dated, attributable assessment for each activity, with the evidence it rests on. Our method follows ISO/IEC 17025, the standard for competence in testing laboratories.

Give people direction

A distance to the next tier computed in named skills, so development is planned against evidence rather than negotiated in adjectives.

Staffing is first deliberately. It is the use that pays for the effort of keeping the records honest, and the one that would collapse first if they went stale.

What we want to achieve4

We are not an accredited laboratory. We align with ISO/IEC 17025 because it is the established approach for this kind of work, not because an external body requires it. That distinction matters: alignment is a choice we can defend on its merits.

What we want to achieve5

The date fields are the ones people forget. Competence is a claim about a point in time. Last practiced is captured separately from assessed, because a level 4 last exercised three years ago is a different fact from one exercised last month.

How a job gets staffed

1. The service

Each of the 17 services lists the competencies it needs and the minimum level for each. Hardware Security Evaluation names 20; a red team engagement names 38.

2. The project

A project opens as a copy of that template, then is tailored to the real engagement: requirements added, dropped, or set to a different level.

3. The match

Every requirement is checked against each person's assessed levels across all domains. Self-assessments are never counted here.

Illustrative counts. The match reads skills across every domain, not just the one the service sits in, so a hardware job needing cryptography depth finds it. When a project closes, the work done on it can feed back as evidence towards assessments.

Methodology7

Dreyfus and Dreyfus, 1980, UC Berkeley. Five stages of directed skill acquisition; the stages differ in kind, not degree. A novice follows rules, an expert reads situations and is often poor at explaining what they just did. Every one of the 278 skills is scored on this same ladder.

Everything turns on the step from 2 to 3

At level 2, supervised

Everything the person produces is reviewed by an authorized colleague before it reaches a client. Their name does not carry the result.

At level 3, authorized

They plan, perform and sign off routine work alone. Nothing stands between their judgement and the client.

What has to happen in between, in this order

Supervised work on real engagements

A passed proficiency assessment

An explicit management authorization

Training alone never closes this gap. Expect most of the laboratory's development effort, and most of its frustration, to sit at exactly this boundary.

Methodology8

ISO/IEC 17025 Clause 6.2.5(e) is where the third step comes from, and we follow it although we are not accredited. Authorization is a deliberate act, not an automatic consequence of crossing a threshold. The software computes; it never promotes.

The criteria are written before the assessment

Differential power analysis, criteria at level 3

  • Designs trace acquisition campaigns (trigger setup, trace count estimation, sampling parameters)
  • Applies appropriate leakage models (HW, HD, identity) and selects optimal attack points
  • Recovers full AES keys from unprotected and lightly protected implementations independently
  • Evaluates attack results using key rank estimation and success metrics
  • Produced DPA/CPA reports on 3+ real engagements meeting lab standards

Self-assessment is heard, not counted

People rate themselves against these same written criteria. Those ratings sit beside the official level with the difference marked, and are excluded from every calculation until a manager ratifies one.

The assessor checks against a standard that already exists, so their judgement is bounded by a document they did not write that day.

Methodology9

A person may state their own view of their competence but not ratify it, because ratifying is what makes the number binding. What matters about the criteria is their shape: specific, observable, countable, fixed in advance. The criteria live in the taxonomy as a controlled document, so changing them is itself logged.

Methodology10

NICE and SFIA are the translation layer: they let us write a job advertisement, answer a tender, or show a partner that our vocabulary connects to a recognised one. No skills framework — NICE, SFIA or ECSF — covers hardware security, which is why that part of the taxonomy was synthesized from CHES and COSADE literature, Common Criteria methodology, FIPS 140-3 and EMVCo.

A profile sets direction. It does not describe a person

What a profile is

A ladder for one domain, with five tiers and the thresholds for each. Eleven exist. It answers one question: inside this domain, what comes next?

Why one is never enough

People accumulate skills across domains. Reading a single profile alone makes somebody look narrower than they are, and hides the cross-domain depth real engagements need.

Methodology11

Illustrative. The rule: use a profile to answer "what should this person work on next", and the whole set of rows to answer "what can this person be put on". Confusing the two is the most common way to misread somebody. Foundations sits underneath all eleven and is not shown, because everybody is measured on it regardless of specialism.

A tier asks four questions

Deep enough?

Enough skills in this domain, held at a high enough level.

Broad enough?

Spread across enough areas of the domain, not all in one corner.

Basics solid?

Report writing, evidence handling, ethics and the rest of the foundations.

Must-haves?

The few named skills that tier requires, with no substitutions.

Four yes, and that is your tier. One no, and the report names which one.

Nothing is scored or weighted, so being exceptional at one thing cannot buy out a gap somewhere else. That matters when the gap is report writing, because the report is what the client actually receives.

Methodology12

The exact arithmetic, if asked: depth is checked at two levels at once, for example sixteen skills at level 3 and seven at level 4. Breadth counts categories where a skill is held at level 2 or above. The foundations check is an average across all eleven foundation skills, unassessed ones counting as zero.

Lead and Principal are both technical tiers

Each requires technical project management and client communication at level 3, because running an evaluation and briefing a client are part of the technical work. Principal extends Lead rather than branching from it: the same requirements, with more depth behind them.

The figures are hardware security. Counts scale with domain size, so a 14-skill domain asks for 7 where a 49-skill one asks for 23, but the shape never changes: Principal repeats Lead's count and Senior's count, each one level higher. Advancing is mostly the same skills deepening, not more skills.

Methodology13

Every tier is reached by assessed capability rather than by appointment. Every Principal threshold already implies the matching Lead threshold, so nobody reaches the fifth tier without having passed through the fourth.

Case study14

No real personnel data appears here. One failing gate is enough, and that is the point of the design rather than a flaw in it. Somebody who is a clear 17 against a required 16 on depth still does not advance, because the second depth count is what distinguishes a broad practitioner from a senior one.

What the gap becomes

Gap computed

Two hardware skills must reach level 4. The system names which are closest.

Plan agreed

Current level, target level, a target date and the actions.

Evidence produced

Engagements chosen to generate what the written criteria ask for.

Re-assessed

Against the same criteria, by a named assessor, on a new date.

A loop, not a yearly event

When a project closes, the work done on it feeds back as evidence towards assessments. That is what keeps the records current instead of letting them rot between review cycles.

Because the actions trace back to written criteria, "get better at side-channel analysis" becomes "second-chair two acquisition campaigns before the end of Q3".

Case study15

Without this loop a competency system is accurate on the day it is populated and wrong six months later. The development plan records current level, target level, target date and agreed actions.

What the system does not measure

What it does measure

  • Assessed capability, per named activity
  • Breadth and depth within one domain
  • Whether authorization was granted, by whom, and on what date

What it does not

  • How good the work actually is
  • How fast somebody is
  • Judgement when an engagement goes wrong at eleven at night
  • Whether a client asks for them again
  • Whether colleagues want to work with them

A person can hold a Principal-level profile and still be difficult to staff. The ladder is a floor on capability, not a judgement of contribution, and it should never be the only input to a performance conversation.

Limitations and exclusions16

Be direct about this, because the temptation to use the ladder as a complete picture of a person is strong and the consequences are unfair. Those judgements remain a manager's job, informed by this record but not replaced by it.

Known compromises, stated plainly

Thresholds are one template, not per-domain evidence

One proportional template scaled to each domain's size, with an identical foundations bar in all eleven. Not calibrated per domain, and not validated against outcomes.

Levels are ordinal, and we average them

Level 4 is not twice level 2. The foundations average works as a gate and would be indefensible as a score.

Decay is recorded but not applied

Nothing reduces a level gone three years unpracticed. Only a person reading the dates will notice.

Breadth against depth is not codified

Tiers are computed per domain, so somebody senior in two domains has no aggregate. That judgement is made, and nothing records or constrains it.

A system that is not honest about its limits invites use it cannot support.

Limitations and exclusions17

The last card is the live question: codifying it would mean defining an exchange rate between domains, which the deck elsewhere says does not exist, so it stays a conversation. The thresholds were also scaled across domains of different sizes. Automatic demotion for decay was rejected as something that should need a human. Two more if there is time: a level is only as good as its assessor, with no second assessor and no calibration between them; and the foundations average counts unassessed skills as zero, so new joiners look worse than they are.

Four things not to do with it

Map levels to pay bands

The scale will not bear that weight, least of all between two different domains.

Compare across domains

A level 3 in cryptography and a level 3 in OSINT are not the same amount of work. There is no exchange rate.

Read a self-rating as performance

It is a person describing their own competence against written criteria, which is a different thing.

Quote a stale level

Check the assessed date and the last-practiced date before using any profile in a decision.

Used for what it measures, this is the most defensible record of capability the laboratory has. Used for anything else, it will mislead.

Limitations and exclusions18

Close here. Offer the companion reading document, which carries the full standards list, the glossary and the complete threshold tables for all eleven ladders. Invite HR to bring back cases where they wanted a number the system would not give them.
